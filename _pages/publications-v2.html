The Role of Firm Risk in Supply Chain Network Adaptation

Under Review

Shailesh J. Divey, M. Hakan Hekimoğlu, and T. Ravichandran.

Minor Revision at Production and Operations Management.

Abstract

This paper examines how firm risk affects supply chain networks, showing that the collective responses of firms to risk can reshape influence and resilience across U.S. manufacturing networks. Using supplier-customer relationship data collected from FactSet pertaining to the US manufacturing industry, we examine the effects of a firm’s risk on its centrality. By establishing firm risk as a determinant of supply chain network structure, the results offer insights for enhancing supply chain resilience. Our findings provide key academic and managerial insights into the effects of risk on a firm’s centrality, which can be interpreted as a proxy for the firm’s importance within the network. From an academic standpoint, our study serves as a foundation block for researchers to further investigate the interplay between idiosyncratic firm risk and clusters of connected firms. From a managerial perspective, we recommend managers pay attention to monitoring the relationships with suppliers and customers in addition to their firms’ ability to diversify their customer/supplier bases.

Keywords: firm risk, centrality, supply chain networks, network adaptation, complex adaptive systems.

Using Spatiotemporal Analysis to Identify Potential Sex Trafficking Victims in Commercial Sex Advertisements

Under Review

N. K. Freeman, Shailesh J. Divey, G. J. Bott, and B. K. Keskin.

Reject & Resubmit at Management Science.

Abstract

In this paper, using text and image data associated with sex ads collected from websites that host advertisements for commercial sex services, we create a curated dataset that is used to construct a graphical representation for all the individuals identified in the data. By leveraging techniques from the field of machine learning and graph theory, we analyze spatiotemporal patterns of these individuals in order to isolate and cluster those that exhibit highly correlated movement patterns across space and time. The information obtained from these clusters provide insights regarding geographical circuits that are likely to be employed by sex traffickers. These insights, in turn, are important for policymakers, law-enforcement, and non-profit organizations for devising strategies to conduct effective countertrafficking operations.

Broken Generic Drug Supply Chains: Evidence from Metformin

Under Review

J. V. Gray, R. Handfield, Shailesh J. Divey, A. Sahebi-Fakhrabad, A. Sadeghi, N. Sharma, P. Butler, B. Teasdale, K. Schulman, and D. Light.

Under Review at Health Affairs.

Abstract

Generic drugs are assumed to be bioequivalent across manufacturers and manufacturing locations. Whether quality varies meaningfully and whether the market responds to such differences remains uncertain. We examine two types of quality: 1) tested drug product quality data from three U.S. market sweeps (2020, 2022, 2024), and 2) manufacturing quality from Food and Drug Administration (FDA) facility inspections. The drugs we study are manufactured in the U.S., India, and China. Volume and price serve are our product market outcomes. We use regression with clustered standard errors to analyze inspection-market and country-quality and relationships, and Spearman correlations for quality-market associations. We find that the worst FDA inspection outcome is associated with the largest market volumes, while prices do not differ by inspection outcome. Market outcomes either do not relate to drug quality, or when they do, worse quality relates to higher price or volume. Drugs made in India have worse tested quality than U.S.-made products. These findings indicate no market incentive for manufacturing compliance or product quality. We discuss policy recommendations to address broken generic drug supply chains.

Live-Streaming and Impulse Buying in E-Commerce: A Dual-Channel Supply Chain

Under Review

B. J. Ma, J. Zhao, Shailesh J. Divey, R. Z. Farahani.

Major Revision at International Journal of Production Economics.

Abstract

Live-streaming e-commerce has rapidly emerged as a powerful sales channel, particularly in sectors such as apparel, beauty, and household goods. Its ability to combine real-time interaction, entertainment value, and time-limited promotions has introduced a new dimension to online retail strategy. However, these same features also stimulate impulsive consumer behavior, which complicates pricing decisions, supply chain coordination, and profitability management. This study examines the strategic implications of impulsive buying behavior in a dual-channel supply chain comprising a manufacturer, a retailer, and two consumer segments: rational and impulsive. Specifically, we develop a game-theoretic model that captures consumer heterogeneity, channel pricing structures, and the value-added effects of live streaming. The model compares the wholesale price and marketplace models under varying degrees of impulsiveness, commission rates, and content quality. Our results yield several counterintuitive insights. First, stronger impulsive behavior does not always enhance profits—it may reduce them due to intensified channel interactions and demand volatility. Second, live-streaming prices may exceed those of ordinary online channels, reflecting the value-added content and behavioral biases associated with live streaming. Third, the wholesale price model may outperform the marketplace model when commission rates and impulsive tendencies are high, as it grants retailers greater pricing autonomy. Finally, rational consumers, often overlooked in promotional strategies, can act as a stabilizing force, helping mitigate channel conflict and restore profitability. These findings suggest several actionable strategies: retailers should avoid over-relying on impulsive demand and instead maintain pricing discipline across channels to reduce cannibalization.

Learning to Comply or Learning to Improve? Organizational Learning and Geography in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

Working Paper

with N. Safaei and J. Gray.

Abstract

Problem definition: Pharmaceutical manufacturers accumulate experience through production and regulatory oversight, but experience may not improve all dimensions of performance equally. We examine whether manufacturers learn to improve patient-facing product quality, learn to perform better on dimensions evaluated by regulators, or both, and whether these relationships differ across manufacturing locations. Methodology/results: We combine longitudinal FDA inspection and citation records, patient-level prescription and healthcare data from the MarketScan Claims database, and facility-location data to follow pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities over time. We use within-facility longitudinal models to relate accumulated production and regulatory feedback to subsequent outcomes, and we develop a two-state framework that separates regulator-facing performance from underlying manufacturing-quality risk and allows improvement in one dimension to transfer imperfectly to the other. Greater production experience is not consistently associated with better patient-facing quality, and repeated inspection exposure alone does not reliably improve regulatory performance. However, adverse regulatory feedback is followed by substantially clearer improvement in subsequent regulator-facing performance than in patient-facing quality. More detailed FDA citation outcomes likewise show little evidence of broad improvement across specific deficiencies. We also find no consistent evidence that these relationships differ between U.S. and major offshore manufacturing locations or with local manufacturing concentration. Together, the findings are consistent with selective learning: organizations may improve most clearly on dimensions receiving salient and direct feedback without comparable improvement across other performance dimensions. Managerial implications: Regulatory compliance and realized product quality should not be treated as interchangeable measures of manufacturing capability. Regulators, manufacturers, and supply-chain decision makers can obtain a more complete assessment of facility performance by combining inspection histories with independent measures of downstream product quality. For operations and organizational-learning research, the findings highlight the importance of distinguishing accumulated experience from informative feedback and of examining where organizational improvement actually occurs.

Information Design with Endogenous Learning in Supply Chains

Working Paper

In preparation for submission to Manufacturing & Service Operations Management.

Abstract

We study information sharing in a supply chain where shared data affects both current production decisions and future forecasting capability. Unlike traditional information-design models that treat disclosure as static belief management, we model data sharing as capability building. We develop an analytical framework combining information disclosure, endogenous forecast learning, and imperfect implementation of forecast-based recommendations. We show that data sharing improves forecast precision, reducing the value of manual disclosure as suppliers rely more on their own signals. We further show that forecast precision and execution reliability are complements, implying that firms should coordinate investments in data sharing, forecasting, and execution.

From Label to Factory Floor: Integrating FDA Data Systems for Pharmaceutical Supply Chain Research

Working Paper

with J. Gray, R. Handfield, A. Sahebi-Fakhrabad, and A. Sadeghi.

Abstract

Empirical research on pharmaceutical supply chains is fundamentally constrained by a fragmented regulatory data landscape. Manufacturer identities, facility registrations, product approvals, and compliance histories are scattered across incompatible federal systems — each with distinct identifiers, inconsistent formatting conventions, and no native crosswalk. The result is that constructing a unified, facility-level view of the U.S. drug supply requires resolving a cascade of non-trivial linkage problems that have, until now, precluded large-scale empirical work in this domain.

We describe the construction of a novel dataset that overcomes these challenges by systematically integrating records across FDA's DailyMed, Orange Book, Drug Establishment Registration, and inspection databases. The result is a comprehensive, facility-level panel connecting every drug product label to its manufacturer, physical production site, regulatory approval history, and cumulative inspection record.

From Regulatory Text to Shortage Risk: An NLP and LLM Framework for Pharmaceutical Quality Risk Prediction

Work in Progress

with J. Gray, R. Handfield, A. Sahebi-Fakhrabad, and A. Sadeghi.

Abstract

Drug shortages driven by manufacturing quality failures impose severe costs on patients, hospitals, and supply chains. Yet the rich unstructured regulatory text that FDA generates like inspection observations, warning letters, recall narratives, and import refusal records, remains largely untapped as a predictive resource. This paper presents a multi-source regulatory intelligence framework that integrates five FDA databases covering 129 pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities across 14 high-priority generic Active Pharmacutical Ingredients (APIs). We construct a facility-level feature matrix that integrates structured regulatory outcomes with NLP-derived text signals, enabling systematic validation and prediction of downstream supply disruptions. An LLM pipeline further extracts structured features from Form 483 PDFs, capturing violation types, escalation patterns, and latent firm behavior through the lens of Agency Theory and Remediation Economics. We then validate these features against independent ground-truth measures, including analytical drug quality testing and pharmacy claims data capturing real-world dispensing disruptions.

Preliminary results reveal actionable early-warning signals: specific regulatory citation patterns predict the most severe inspection outcomes (Pearson correlation = 0.51), contamination and manufacturing practice failures account for 94% of drug recalls linked to our study compounds, and import shipments rejected after laboratory testing concentrate in a small set of high-risk facilities. These signals can be detected month before shortages materialize, enabling proactive supply chain risk management.

The resulting index provides a tool for proactive supplier risk stratification and informing sourcing strategies in pharmaceutical supply chains. To our knowledge, this is the first study linking FDA regulatory inspection text to pharmacy claims data for supply disruption risk.
